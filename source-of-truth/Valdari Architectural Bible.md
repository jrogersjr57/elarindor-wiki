# The Seven Principles of Valdari Architecture

1. **Stone endures.** Every permanent building begins on a carefully laid stone foundation.
2. **Wood is honored.** Timber is shaped to work with its natural grain, not against it.
3. **Joinery is craftsmanship.** Precision joints and hardwood pegs are preferred over iron nails wherever practical.
4. **Crafted for generations.** A home is expected to outlive the family that first builds it.
5. **Nature is welcomed.** Buildings belong to the landscape rather than dominating it.
6. **Beauty follows purpose.** Decoration grows from craftsmanship instead of being applied afterward.
7. **Every building tells its story.** A blacksmith’s forge, a beekeeper’s cottage, or an elder’s home should each reveal their purpose through their design.

# Common Characteristics of Valdari Buildings

## Foundations, Porches, and Building Purpose

Every permanent Valdari building begins on a carefully laid stone
foundation, but this does **not** require every building to stand on a
tall raised stone base.

Foundation height and form respond to practical conditions such as
terrain, drainage, climate, soil, and building purpose. Some buildings
may have substantial raised stone foundations, while others may use a
low foundation and sit much closer to surrounding ground level.

Porches are not a universal requirement of Valdari architecture.
Family homes may commonly use porches as sheltered outdoor and gathering
spaces, but workshops, shops, forges, storage buildings, and other
purpose-built structures should follow their practical needs. A
blacksmith's building, for example, need not have a porch simply to look
Valdari.

Mixed-use buildings are a valid direction for further development.
A craftsperson or merchant may potentially live above or alongside a
workplace, but the prevalence, layout, and social implications of such
arrangements remain under development.

## Construction Materials and Trade

Valdari architecture may use both locally available and traded
construction materials.

A settlement does not need to possess forests, suitable building stone,
or every other construction resource within its immediate territory.
Materials unavailable locally may be obtained through trade with other
Valdari communities or with neighboring peoples.

Wood may therefore come from forest communities or other timber-producing
regions rather than being harvested beside every settlement. Building
stone may likewise be quarried elsewhere and transported to communities
that lack suitable local stone.

The specific sources of major building materials, regional trade
networks, and the roles of dwarves, humans, or other peoples in supplying
stone or other materials remain **TBD** unless established elsewhere.

Architectural identity should therefore arise from Valdari construction
principles, craftsmanship, joinery, durability, scale, adaptation to
place, and purposeful design rather than requiring identical local
materials or identical building forms in every region.

- One to one-and-a-half stories for most homes.
- Carefully laid stone foundations; foundation height varies with terrain, drainage, climate, and building purpose.
- Hand-hewn timber construction.
- Precision wooden joinery with hardwood pegs.
- Steep cedar or hardwood shingle roofs.
- Deep overhanging eaves.
- Porches and covered outdoor spaces where appropriate to building function; common on some homes but not universal.
- Warm natural wood finishes rather than painted exteriors.
- Carvings reserved for meaningful structural elements.
- Buildings nestled into the landscape rather than dominating it.

## Landscape Integration and Vegetation

Valdari architecture belongs within the surrounding landscape, but integration with nature does not mean covering buildings in vegetation.

The Valdari generally achieve landscape integration through:

- thoughtful siting and adaptation to terrain,
- building scale and proportion,
- use of natural and appropriately sourced materials,
- paths, gardens, working land, and planted areas surrounding buildings,
- preservation or deliberate placement of useful shade and vegetation,
- and construction that responds to local drainage, weather, and environmental conditions.

Vines, flowers, moss, and other plants may occur on or immediately beside buildings where appropriate, but they are not a defining architectural motif and should not be applied decoratively to every structure.

Valdari buildings should not routinely appear overgrown, flower-draped, vine-covered, or visually absorbed into surrounding vegetation merely to communicate the Valdari relationship with nature.

The architecture itself should remain clearly legible. Timber grain, joinery, hardwood pegs, stonework, roof construction, functional openings, weathering, maintenance, and purposeful craftsmanship should carry more of the building’s visual identity than botanical ornament.

Where vegetation would interfere with access, maintenance, drainage, ventilation, craftwork, commerce, animal care, or another building function, practical needs take precedence.

Landscape integration is ecological and architectural rather than ornamental overgrowth.

# Architectural Exclusions

Current Valdari architecture is deliberately constructed.

Valdari buildings should not be treated as:

- houses grown from living trees,
- homes built primarily inside hollow trees or roots,
- treehouse cities.

For replaced architectural directions and their development context, see [[Worldbuilding/Elarindor/AI Sources of Truth/Elarindor Development History]].
